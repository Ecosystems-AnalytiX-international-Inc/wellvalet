Is "May Contain" Regulated in Canada? What Every Allergy Parent Should Know

10 September 2026 · 5 min read

A box of cookies says "may contain traces of peanut." A parent standing in the aisle has about three seconds to decide what that actually means: is it a real warning backed by testing, or a defensive line the manufacturer's lawyers asked for just in case? The honest answer is unsettling — in Canada, nobody is required to check, and nobody is required to put it there in the first place.

That single fact changes how you should read every "may contain" label you'll ever see.

The short answer: no, it isn't regulated

The Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) is the body that sets and enforces food labelling rules in Canada. Its own industry guidance describes precautionary statements as being used "when a food allergen or gluten source may be unintentionally present" — and recommends manufacturers use the wording "May contain [allergen]." Recommends. Not requires.

What CFIA guidance actually says: cross-contamination (precautionary) statements are a voluntary industry practice, not a mandatory labelling requirement. There is no standardized testing threshold in Canadian law that tells a manufacturer when a "may contain" statement is required versus optional — that call is left entirely to the company making the product.

What IS regulated — and why the distinction matters

It's easy to lump every allergen-related line on a package together, but Canadian rules actually draw a hard line between two very different things:

  • Deliberate ingredients (mandatory): if an allergen is genuinely part of the recipe, it must be declared by its common name in the ingredient list, and again in a "Contains" statement — "milk" instead of casein, "egg" instead of albumin. CFIA enforces this.
  • Accidental cross-contact (voluntary): if an allergen might show up because it's processed on shared equipment or in a shared facility, the manufacturer can choose to add a "may contain" statement — or not. Nobody checks their decision, and no two companies apply the same standard.

The result is a label that looks like a single, consistent safety system but is actually two systems duct-taped together: one that's enforced, and one that's entirely voluntary.

Why some manufacturers skip the warning, and others slap it on everything

Once you know precautionary labelling is unregulated, the inconsistency on grocery shelves stops being surprising. Some manufacturers use "may contain" defensively, on nearly every product that shares a production line with a major allergen — a legally safe choice for them, but one that quietly shrinks the pool of products an allergic shopper feels comfortable buying, whether or not the actual risk is meaningful. Others under-use it, running facilities with genuine cross-contact risk but no formal requirement — or internal process — that forces a warning onto the label at all.

Neither pattern is illegal. Both make the label harder to trust at face value, which is exactly why Health Canada's own food allergy resources and groups like Food Allergy Canada push families toward a more cautious reading of what's printed, rather than a literal one.

What allergy families should actually do with a "may contain" warning

Food Allergy Canada's guidance on this is direct: treat a "may contain" statement as a reason to avoid the product, not as a risk to weigh yourself. That's a hard recommendation precisely because there's no regulated testing behind the wording — you have no way to know if "may contain peanut" means trace-level shared-equipment risk or something more significant, because the label doesn't have to say.

The same caution applies in reverse: a product with no precautionary statement isn't automatically guaranteed allergen-free either, particularly for a newly reformulated product or one from a smaller manufacturer without a mature labelling process. If you're genuinely unsure, Food Allergy Canada's advice is to contact the manufacturer directly rather than guess.

Frequently asked questions

Is a "may contain" statement a legal requirement in Canada?
No. CFIA recommends the wording for manufacturers who choose to use precautionary labelling, but using one at all is voluntary. What's mandatory is declaring real ingredients — priority allergens actually used in the product — in the ingredient list and a "Contains" statement.

Who regulates food allergen labelling in Canada?
The Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) sets and enforces labelling requirements, working from food safety policy set by Health Canada.

What's the difference between a "Contains" statement and a "may contain" statement?
"Contains" discloses a deliberate ingredient and is mandatory. "May contain" warns about accidental cross-contact and is voluntary and unregulated, so it varies from one manufacturer to the next.

Should I trust a product with no "may contain" warning?
Not automatically. Because the practice is voluntary with no standardized threshold, some real cross-contact risk goes unlabelled. Contact the manufacturer directly if you're unsure.
